The NYC Immigrant Enclaves map

What the record establishes, and what it does not

Published August 21, 2026, 1:12 PM EDT. Every live value on this page was checked on or before that date. The sources it describes can change after it, and one of them — the page that carried the map — already has.

This article accompanies the NYC Immigrant Enclaves public view. Every fact it restates is held there as a governed claim; the article cites those claims and holds none of its own.

This collection governs a map as an artifact. It does not govern New York City's immigrant neighborhoods, and it takes no position on which neighborhoods belong on any such map. The distinction is the whole point: the questions that proved answerable here were about who published a document, what it claims, and how confidently any of that can be said -- not about the city it depicts.

The map was chosen as a subject because its publication details are not stated on the artifact itself. It is titled New York City Immigrant Enclaves, it lists thirty neighborhoods, and it carries a footnote naming the Mayor's Office of Immigrant Affairs as its source. It carries no publisher mark, no date, and no address of its own. A footnote naming a data source records where information came from; it does not identify who produced the artifact. The map's publication is established here; its authorship is not.

In July 2026 the map drew wide public comment because Little Italy is not on it, and the mayor said additional changes would be made, including adding Little Italy. That commitment is recorded here. The amendment is not, because no amended map has been observed. The two are different facts, and this collection records them separately.

Earlier passes of this intake merged the two, recording the commitment as a completed change that had not been observed -- one of four claims retracted in a single working session. The record that survived was shaped so the same compression could not recur: it carries no supersession link, and the map record it would supersede is not marked superseded.

The same reading appears outside this intake. When the question was re-tested before this article was written, two independent third-party sources were found to have reported it the same way: a news outlet headlined a completed restoration over a body reading that changes would be made in the future, beneath an image captioned as the map that sparked the controversy; an advocacy organization headlined that the neighborhood was back on the map over a body reading that it would be added. Neither showed or linked an amended map. That reading appeared in independent third-party sources, not only in this intake.

What the re-test could establish, it established by retrieval rather than inference. The map file at its known address is byte-for-byte identical to its earliest archived capture and to a fresh retrieval; the file that was live three weeks before the public comment is the file that is live now. The publisher's sitemap of more than ten thousand pages contains no enclave-map page, and the program page it lists now redirects. The only other map-named image on the surviving program page was fetched and looked at rather than judged by its filename, and proved to be an unrelated official visitor map.

What it could not establish is stated as plainly. Whether an amended map exists at an address nothing links to remains unknown. The one query that could have found such a map -- a sweep of the publisher's entire media library -- could not be run: it returned nothing against a search space known to contain results, so its silence proves nothing. That query is recorded as uncovered rather than as a negative finding. The amendment has not been observed, which is not the same as establishing that it did not happen.

The selection criteria were the hardest part of the collection to model. Reporting attributes two different rationales to the map: a demographic threshold and a heritage-based rationale. They look like a contradiction and they are not one. They ask different questions, and a record classifying the apparent conflict as a difference in definition asserts precisely that the appearance was examined and the cause is a mismatch of scope -- not that the two sources disagree. Classifying a real conflict as a mismatch is the failure mode this record is checked against, and the classification was made on the evidence rather than by default.

Confidence values in this collection carry a stated basis rather than a bare number. Evidential distance sets a ceiling and never sets the value: a claim resting on one outlet's report of a press conference cannot be recorded as confidently as a checksummed file this collection retrieved itself, however plausible it sounds. The amendment commitment sits below the outlet ceiling because the same article paraphrases an adjacent claim it could have quoted, which bears on how precisely it reports.

Unresolved questions are recorded as unresolved. The report contains an internal inconsistency, recorded as observed rather than resolved. One source's attribution is contested between two city offices by two outlets, and that disagreement is absent from the governed records because the second citation could not be resolved to a retrievable document. An unresolved question is kept as an unresolved question.

Rights were determined before publication rather than assumed. Municipal works are not public domain -- the federal rule does not reach city governments -- and the city's terms reserve all rights. Where an outlet asserts no machine-readable license, the position is recorded as undetermined, which is neither absent nor permissive. Public accessibility is not permission, so this collection retains checksums and content-addressed addresses rather than redistributing bytes it has no permission to redistribute.
