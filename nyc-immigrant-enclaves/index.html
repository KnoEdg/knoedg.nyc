New York City Immigrant Enclaves

In July 2026 a map of New York City's immigrant enclaves drew wide public comment because Little Italy is not on it. Almost everything written about it since has had to name a publisher and a date in its first sentence, and the accounts do not agree with one another.

This page holds what can be established about that map, and states what cannot. It is built from 8 governed records, each traceable to a retrieved document. Where a question has no answer in the evidence, the answer here is that it has no answer — not a plausible reconstruction.

A data paper discusses this collection at length: The NYC Immigrant Enclaves map. It cites the claims on this page and holds none of its own.

Published August 21, 2026, 9:00 AM EDT. Every live value on this page was checked on or before that date. The sources it describes can change after it, and one of them — the page that carried the map — already has.

The map

A map titled “NEW YORK CITY IMMIGRANT ENCLAVES”, footnoted “Source: NYC Mayor’s Office of Immigrant Affairs”, lists 30 numbered enclaves with transit access and does not include Little Italy. It was published by New York City Tourism + Conventions, established from its host page’s own source rather than from press attribution.

The footnote is a data-source citation, not a byline. It credits an office as the source of the information; it does not say who made the map. The file itself carries no publisher mark, no date, no designer credit, and no authorship metadata of any kind.

Where the list came from

The Mayor’s Office of Immigrant Affairs published an Immigrant Enclave Illustration Series from 2023-04-17, and states in its own end-of-tenure report that 23 illustrations were published. “Little Italy” does not appear anywhere in that 85-page report.

Comparing the two, exactly five names appear on the map and nowhere in that report: Little Egypt, Little Odessa, Little Palestine, Little Poland, Little Senegal. The remaining difference is structural. The map is an extension of the series, not a subset of it. Who made the additions is not established.

Four of the five added names refer to communities at the center of contested public argument. No source found identifies who added them, and this page does not guess. The difference between the two documents is a fact about the documents; its authorship is not.

Two criteria that are not in conflict

Two selection criteria are usually reported as opposing answers to one question: whether Little Italy belongs on the map. They are not opposing answers. They are different questions.

The demographic criterion. A city spokesperson, quoted by one outlet, said the map “highlights neighborhoods in New York City that have substantial foreign-born populations from regions and countries around the world.” This criterion asks how many residents of a neighborhood today were born abroad. It appears in no published rule.

The heritage criterion. Critics, quoted by the press, treat a neighborhood’s history of immigrant settlement as the basis for inclusion, regardless of how many residents were born abroad today. This criterion also appears in no published rule.

A neighborhood can satisfy either, both, or neither, and both can be true of the same place at once. Neither criterion was ever published as a rule. Both exist only as statements made to reporters, which is why no adjudication between them is possible and none is attempted here.

This collection records the relationship between them explicitly rather than leaving it to silence: the apparent conflict was examined, and its cause is recorded as a difference in what each criterion is about.

A published threshold, and what it does not explain

A published threshold does exist elsewhere. The Department of City Planning’s 2026 report defines a “major immigrant neighborhood” as one whose foreign-born density exceeds the city average, using a threshold of 40 percent, and records SoHo-Little Italy at 30.5 percent. The report scopes that definition to its own chapter, and nothing found connects it to this map.

This is the point at which a reader is most likely to draw a conclusion the evidence does not support. A published threshold exists; a neighborhood falls below it; the neighborhood is missing from a map. That sequence is not a chain of cause. Nothing found shows the map's authors read the report, cited it, or applied any threshold at all.

It appears here because leaving it out would be its own distortion. The absence of a link is on the record, rather than assumed in either direction.

What happened next

On 2026-07-10 the mayor said the map was “initially created by the prior administration in 2023” and that “we’re going to be making additional changes in the future … and that includes adding Little Italy to the map.” That is a commitment to a future change. No amended version has been located.

Checked on 2026-08-20, the page that carried the map redirects away and its replacement mentions neither the program nor the map, while the map file itself is still served. Why the page was withdrawn is not established.

A stated commitment and an observed change are recorded separately. As of the date this page was issued the map still lists 30 entries and Little Italy is not among them. This collection records the commitment and does not record an amendment, because none has been observed.

What is not established

Stated plainly, because a collection that only records what it found misrepresents itself:

  • Who designed the map. No source names a designer for this artifact.
  • Who chose the five added names, and on whose authority.
  • Whether any selection criterion was applied at all, published or otherwise.
  • Why the page was withdrawn. A program page ending with its program is ordinary; nothing dates the withdrawal against the public comment.
  • Whether the stated addition has been made. No amended version has been located.

Limitations

This collection holds claims about the guide as an artifact. It is not a directory of New York City's immigrant neighborhoods, and nothing here should be read as endorsing or disputing any neighborhood's inclusion or exclusion.

One cited source's attribution is contested between two city offices by two outlets. That disagreement is not asserted in the governed records, because the second outlet's citation could not be resolved to a retrievable document.

The report contains an internal inconsistency: a repeated caption means its stated count of 23 may enumerate one fewer distinct enclave. It is recorded as observed and not reconciled.

Whether an amended map exists at an address nothing links to has not been determined. Re-checked on 2026-08-20: the map file at its known address is byte-for-byte unchanged, the publisher's sitemap of 10,075 pages contains no enclave-map page, and no retrieved reporting shows or links an amended map. A sweep of the publisher's entire media library could not be run, so that space is uncovered. The amendment has not been observed, which is not the same as establishing that it did not happen.
